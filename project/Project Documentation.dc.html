2. Introduction and background

A district hospital in Ghana handles a few hundred outpatients on a busy day, and almost every one of them is recorded on paper. The folder is retrieved from a shelf, carried by hand between the front desk, the consulting room, the laboratory and the pharmacy, and returned to the shelf at the end of the day, assuming it comes back at all.

Around that folder sits a set of parallel records that do not talk to each other. Billing lives in a cash book or a spreadsheet. Pharmacy stock lives in a stock register. Laboratory requests are slips of paper. Insurance claims are compiled at month end from whatever documentation can be found. Each of these is maintained carefully by somebody, and none of them can be reconciled with the others without a person sitting down and doing it by hand.

This project builds the system that replaces that arrangement: one shared record that every department writes to and reads from, with a patient-facing application so patients are not the last to know what is happening in their own care.

We chose this problem for three reasons. It is real, so we could go and watch it happen. It is broad enough to exercise the whole software engineering process from requirements to deployment. And the consequences of getting it wrong are serious enough to force discipline: a system that confuses two patients' records is worse than no system at all.

3. Problem statement

Hospital information at facility level is fragmented across paper and disconnected records, and the fragmentation itself causes harm.

  1. Records are lost or duplicated. A misplaced folder means a clinician works without history. A duplicate registration means one patient has two histories and neither is complete.
  2. Results arrive late or not at all. A result on a slip of paper reaches the ordering doctor when somebody carries it. A critical result travelling at walking pace is a clinical risk.
  3. Prescriptions are transcribed by hand. Handwriting is misread, and allergies recorded in a folder the pharmacist has never seen are not checked at the point of dispensing.
  4. Charges are missed. Services delivered in one department are billed from another, using documentation that may not have arrived. Revenue already earned is never collected.
  5. Claims are late and incomplete. Compiling claims retrospectively from paper produces rejections, and rejections are rarely appealed because the evidence must be found again.
  6. Management is blind. There is no reliable same-day figure for bed occupancy, throughput or outstanding revenue, so decisions are made on impression.
  7. Patients wait without information. Booking requires attending in person, results require returning in person, and bills are learned at the cash desk.

The common cause is that no single record exists which all parties can see. Every problem above is a symptom of that one absence.

8. System analysis

Analysis of the current process produced three findings that determined the shape of the system.

FindingConsequence for the design

9. System design, summary

Full document: Design_Documentation.pdf, eleven sections, ten figures: architecture, use case, class, three sequence diagrams, swimlane activity, component, entity relationship and wireframes.

Twenty-two use cases across nine actors. The most complex, conducting a consultation, is specified in full with three alternate flows: a blocked prescription, a missing diagnosis, and an unavailable AI service. Each alternate flow corresponds to a rule the system enforces, not an error it merely reports.

The class model is composed from Patient, which owns its clinical history. Order lifecycles are modelled as forward-only state machines, so a laboratory sample cannot jump from ordered to verified without passing through collection and processing. That constraint exists because the record must be honest about where a sample actually is.

10. Architecture

Four layers with dependencies pointing downward only. The persistence interface is owned by the domain, so the storage implementation can be replaced without touching any layer above it. That property is what made the database migration a swap rather than a rewrite, and §13.3 reports how it held in practice.

We chose a layered design over microservices deliberately. Signing a consultation stages a laboratory order, a prescription and an invoice line in one action. Splitting that across services would require distributed transactions for an ordinary clinical event, and would produce a deployment a hospital IT office could not reasonably run. The module boundaries are drawn so a service split remains possible if patient volume ever demands it.

11. Database and data design

Sixteen entities: fourteen tables plus two log tables. Patient is the hub of the clinical record and Invoice the hub of the financial record. Five design decisions encode requirements, not preferences.

  • MRN is the patient key, because staff already speak in MRNs and translating between the system and the folder label invites error.
  • Invoice status is derived from total and paid, never stored, so the two cannot disagree after a partial payment.
  • Bed occupancy is a nullable patient reference on the bed row with a unique constraint, so double allocation is impossible at the data level, and not only in the interface.
  • Allergies sit on the patient row instead of a separate table, because they are read on every prescription check and never queried independently. A join on the hottest safety path is a cost with no benefit.
  • The audit table is append-only. No update or delete permission is granted on it, including to administrators.

Every order, whether a test, a scan or a prescription, references the encounter that created it. That is what an auditor or an insurer asks for, and reconstructing it later from timestamps is unreliable.

12. User interface design

Seven principles governed the interface. The two that shaped it most: every clinical role lands on the work waiting for them, not a menu of links, and every status carries both a colour and a word so it survives printing and colour blindness. Clinical values use a monospaced typeface so digits align and misreadings are less likely. AI output is always purple, so an assistive suggestion is never visually confusable with recorded fact.

Safety warnings interrupt. They do not merely inform. An allergy conflict blocks the action and states the reason in place; a critical result appears as a banner at the top of the ordering doctor's dashboard. A warning that can be scrolled past is not a warning.

13.3 How the problems in §3 were addressed in code

ProblemImplementation that addresses it
Lost or duplicated recordsOne record keyed on a system-allocated MRN. Search by name or MRN returns matches as the user types, so a duplicate registration is visible before it is created.
Results arriving lateVerification releases the result simultaneously to the chart, the ordering doctor and the patient's application. A critical flag raises a banner on the doctor's dashboard instead of waiting to be noticed.
Misread prescriptions and unchecked allergiesElectronic prescribing removes handwriting. A deterministic rule engine blocks any drug matching a recorded allergy and warns on drug-drug interactions before the order can be staged.
Missed chargesCharge capture fires from the clinical event itself. Ordering a test, signing a consultation and dispensing a drug each append an invoice line at that moment.
Late or incomplete claimsA claim record exists per insured invoice from the outset and moves through submitted, authorised and paid, with an AI-drafted justification available.
Blind managementDashboard KPIs computed from live state, a streaming activity feed, and an assistant that answers plain-language questions from current data only.
Patients without informationMobile booking against real availability, verified results, prescription status and bill payment, all self-service.

13.4 AI implementation and its boundaries

Six features call a language model. Two further safety checks are deterministic rule engines, not AI, and that distinction is deliberate: a safety block must be predictable and reproducible, never probabilistic.

FeatureKindBehaviour and constraint
Consultation co-pilotLanguage modelDrafts a diagnosis and plan from complaint, vitals and history into editable fields. The doctor accepts, edits or discards.
Result explainer, clinicianLanguage modelInterprets a result against its reference range on request.
Result explainer, patientLanguage modelPlain-language explanation with a standing notice that it is not a diagnosis.
Claims assistantLanguage modelDrafts a claim justification for the cashier to check and correct.
Operations copilotLanguage modelAnswers administrator questions from a snapshot of current system data only, never from outside knowledge.
Symptom checkerLanguage modelSuggests a specialty and an urgency band. Never diagnoses, never prescribes, and directs emergencies to the emergency line.
Allergy and interaction guardDeterministic rulesBlocks contraindicated drugs. Not AI, because the outcome must be identical every time.
Triage acuity suggestionDeterministic rulesThreshold bands over the recorded vitals. Transparent and auditable.

Two constraints apply to all six AI features. Every output requires a human to accept it before it enters the record, and failure of the service never blocks a clinical workflow: the system says so and the user proceeds manually.

13.5 Patient mobile application

Built as a separate application against the same domain model. Four onboarding screens on first run, then authentication with a remembered session. Five tabs: home with the next appointment and active medications, booking against real slot availability, records with results, medications and bills, alerts, and profile. Mobile Money payment runs the full provider request and approval sequence and settles the invoice the cashier sees. Touch targets are at least 44 pixels and the layout assumes one hand in poor light.

13.6 Error handling and validation

Class of errorHow the system behaves
Missing mandatory inputSubmission refused, the offending field named. No silent partial save.
Implausible clinical valueRejected with the acceptable range stated, so the user can tell a typo from a real reading.
Illegal state transitionNot offered. Only the next legal stage is presented, so the error cannot be made.
Conflicting write, such as a taken slot or an occupied bedRefused with the conflict explained. Availability is recomputed from current state, not cached.
Unsafe clinical actionBlocked, with the specific allergy or interaction named. Not bypassable from the screen.
External service failureCaught, reported plainly, and the workflow continues without it.
Every successful writeVisible confirmation within a second, so staff never wonder whether it saved.

Fourteen defects were found and all are closed: three critical, six major, five minor. Testing was risk-based, concentrating on the three paths where a hospital system can cause real harm: a wrong drug reaching a patient, a critical result not reaching a doctor, and one patient's record appearing under another's name.

Two lessons are recorded honestly in the report. We tested persistence late, which is why refreshing the browser lost a session's work until it was fixed. 18. Security considerations

ControlImplementation in v2.0
AuthenticationAccounts are rows with argon2id password hashes. Sign-in returns a signed token with a twelve-hour lifetime; a wrong password and an unknown address give the same message, so the system cannot be used to discover which accounts exist.
Session handlingThe browser posts credentials to the web tier, not the API, and the token is stored in an httpOnly cookie. No script on any page can read it. Signing out clears it server-side.
AuthorisationA guard on every API route, checked against a role re-read from the database on each request, never trusted from the token. Non-clinical roles receive an explicit restriction notice in place of clinical data, not merely a hidden menu item.
Least privilegeReceptionist and cashier see billing but not charts the same chart request returns demographics and invoices for them and the full record for a clinician. A patient sees only their own record, and only results a technician has verified.
Audit trailEvery state-changing action recorded with actor, action, target and timestamp. Append-only by construction: the API exposes no update or delete route on the resource anywhere, and a test asserts that none appears.
Credential hygienePasswords masked by default and never written to logs. No secret in the repository; the two that exist live in Secrets Manager. There is no AI key at all, because Bedrock is reached through the compute role.
Password lifecycleAnyone can change their own password, and is asked for the current one first, so a session left open on a ward computer cannot be used to lock its owner out. An administrator can issue a replacement but can never read the old one, because only a hash is stored. A forgotten password is settled at the front desk against photo identification: this system sends no email, and a reset link that never arrives is worse than no feature at all.
Withdrawing accessDeactivation blocks sign-in and clears the password while keeping the staff record, because orders, prescriptions and audit entries all name the person who performed them. No interface anywhere deletes a member of staff, and an administrator cannot deactivate or demote their own account.
Network exposureThe database accepts no connection from any internet address range. The browser reaches one origin and never learns the API's address.
TransportHTTPS only, no mixed content.
Data protectionProcessing aligned to the Data Protection Act, 2012 (Act 843). Clinical records are not deletable through any interface action.
AI data minimisationOnly the clinical context needed for the task is sent. No bulk record transfer. The claims assistant is sent billed lines and never the clinical record.

What changed since v1.0, and what it cost. The previous revision named this section as the honest weakness: authorisation was enforced by code the client ran, so the deployment had to be treated as a demonstration, not a system that could hold real patient data. That weakness is closed. The client now holds nothing but a cookie it cannot read, and every decision about who may see what is taken in a process the user has no access to.

The enforcement point is the API, not the database. Row-level security was built first, tested against a real PostgreSQL instance, and then deliberately replaced. §9.4 of the design document sets out the reasoning. The cost of that choice is specific and worth stating: the database will now hand any row to whatever connects to it, so a route without a guard is a data leak rather than a bug. That is why the authorisation tests are not an afterthought but the artefact that carries the guarantee, and why their file says so in its opening lines.

What remains true. The system holds synthetic data only. A production deployment for real patients would need penetration testing by someone who did not write it, a documented breach procedure, and and a review against Act 843 by someone qualified to give it. A capstone can supply none of those, and we claim none of them.

21. Maintenance strategy, summary

Full document: Maintenance_and_Evolution.pdf, ten sections.

Four kinds of maintenance are planned for. Corrective work is graded P1 to P4 by response priority, taking account of whether a workaround exists; a P1 is fixed the same day. Adaptive work responds to changes outside the system, such as an NHIS format change or a Mobile Money API change, and in both cases the work is confined to reference data or a single adapter. Perfective work comes from users, and four improvements are already identified from usability and acceptance sessions. Preventive work is scheduled rather than triggered: monthly dependency checks, quarterly restore rehearsals, monthly audit review and a six-monthly pharmacist review of the safety rules.

The most important piece of post-submission work is the database migration, set out in six steps with acceptance criteria for each: create the schema with its constraints, move authorisation into row-level security, write the server adapter, switch the adapter at its single construction point, move AI calls server-side so no key reaches a browser, and prove backup by performing a restore. Because the repository interface belongs to the domain, steps 3 and 4 change no screen and no domain class.

The sequencing is deliberate and not negotiable: nothing in v2.5 or v3.0 begins before v2.0 is complete. Every later item increases the volume of real patient data flowing through the system, and increasing that flow while authorisation still lives in the application layer would multiply exposure rather than benefit. Infrastructure before visible features, in this domain, is a safety decision.

Further AI candidates are ranked by how well the value is understood, not by how impressive they sound. No-show prediction and stock demand forecasting are high confidence and low clinical risk, so they come first. Deterioration early warning is valuable and genuinely risky, and would require validation against real outcomes and a clinician's sign-off. Autonomous clinical decision-making is excluded at every release; the requirement for a human to accept every AI output is a permanent design constraint, not a temporary limitation.

23. Conclusion

We set out to replace a fragmented paper process with one shared record, and the system does that. All nine roles work end to end: a patient can be registered at the front desk, triaged, consulted, sent for tests, given drugs, admitted, discharged and billed, with every department reading and writing the same record and every action attributable to a named user.

Two parts are worth more than the feature count. The first is that the safety-critical paths are enforced, not advised: a contraindicated drug cannot be staged, a result cannot reach a patient before it is verified, a bed cannot hold two patients, and a consultation cannot be signed without a diagnosis. Those constraints came directly from the problems we observed. The second is that the architecture anticipated its own biggest gap. The persistence seam was built before we needed it, which is why moving to a real database is a documented swap rather than a rewrite.

The system is not finished, and §20 says where. Authorisation belongs in a database and is not there yet; Mobile Money settlement is modelled; the test suite is manual. None of these is hidden in this documentation, because a maintainer who inherits an honest account of a system's weaknesses is in a far better position than one who inherits a confident one.

What we would tell the next group doing this: build the persistence layer before the screens depend on it, test the deployed build from the first week, and decide early which safety checks must be deterministic. Those three choices accounted for most of the rework we did and most of the confidence we ended with.
